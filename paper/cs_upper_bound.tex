\begin{abstract}
Take two long random strings of 0s and 1s. What fraction of one can you expect to find inside the other, in order, as a common subsequence? The answer per character converges to a constant, $\gamma_2$, whose exact value has been open since 1975. The best proven range was $0.792665 \le \gamma_2 \le 0.826280$, and the upper bound had not moved since 2009. We prove $\gamma_2 \le 0.824443$. The proof is a finite certificate: a table of positive integers, one per state of a finite machine that reads the two strings, satisfying one locally checkable inequality at every state. Finding the table took a day of compute on one desktop machine. Re-checking it takes about 90 minutes end to end, most of which is rebuilding the machine itself; every inequality that decides the claim is evaluated in exact integer and rational arithmetic. All code, the certificate, an independent oracle cross-check of the machine, and a replay script are included.
\end{abstract}

\section{The problem}

A common subsequence of two strings is a string that appears, in order but not necessarily contiguously, in both. The length of the longest common subsequence (LCS) is the standard measure of similarity behind \texttt{diff}, version control, and DNA sequence comparison. Chv\'atal and Sankoff \cite{chvatal1975} asked in 1975: for two uniformly random binary strings of length $n$, how long is the LCS? They proved the expected length divided by $n$ converges to a constant $\gamma_2$, now called the Chv\'atal--Sankoff constant, and this constant calibrates what an ``interesting'' alignment is: a match is meaningful only if it beats what randomness alone produces.

The value of $\gamma_2$ is unknown. Simulations put it near $0.8122$ \cite{boutet1999}. Steele conjectured $2/(1+\sqrt2)\approx 0.8284$ \cite{steele1986}; Lueker \cite{lueker2009} disproved this in 2009 by proving $\gamma_2 \le 0.826280$, alongside a lower bound later raised to $0.792665992$ \cite{heineman2024}. Since 2009 all published progress, including recent computer- and AI-assisted work, has attacked the lower bound. The upper bound has stood untouched for seventeen years.

\section{Result}

\begin{center}
\fbox{\parbox{0.86\linewidth}{\centering\textbf{Theorem.}\quad $\displaystyle \gamma_2 \;\le\; \frac{824443}{1000000} \;=\; 0.824443.$}}
\end{center}

The bound improves the previous record by $0.001837$ and closes about $5.4\%$ of the remaining gap between the proven bounds.

\section{Method}

We use Lueker's own upper-bound machinery \cite{lueker2009}, unchanged, and push it to a machine size that was out of reach in 2009. No new mathematics is introduced; the contribution is a verified computation at a scale two steps beyond the original, and the engineering that makes that scale fit on one desktop computer.

\textbf{The certificate idea, in plain words.} Consider a finite-state machine that reads the two random strings and remembers only the last $h$ characters of each. Lueker showed (his equation~(41) and Theorem~3.3) that if one can exhibit a vector $v$ of positive numbers, one entry per machine state, a weight $0<z<\tfrac12$, and a number $0<\lambda<1$ such that a single linear inequality
\[
A_z\, v \;\le\; \lambda\, v
\]
holds coordinate-by-coordinate (where $A_z$ is the machine's transition operator with matches weighted by $z$), then
\[
\gamma_2 \;\le\; \frac{2\log(2z)}{\log \lambda}.
\]
The vector $v$ is the entire proof. Its correctness does not depend on how it was found: anyone can recheck the inequality row by row.

\textbf{Finding the vector.} Larger $h$ gives a stronger bound but exponentially more states. We work at $h=10$: 400{,}253{,}317 states after reduction, with $2(2h-1)=38$ transition columns. The vector is found by power iteration (repeatedly applying $A_z$ and renormalizing), run for 113 sweeps. Two things make this fit in 128\,GB of memory: states are stored once per class of Lueker's simultaneous bit-complement symmetry (the top/bottom exchange is deliberately \emph{not} quotiented, because the canonical tie-breaking in the state encoding does not respect it), and the vector is held in 32-bit fixed-point integers, so the feasibility test is a statement about integers rather than floats. The weight $z$ was chosen by a grid search over 19 values; the reported certificate uses $z=3209/10000$, the best grid point, which beat its neighbours by about $4\times10^{-8}$ in the resulting bound. We do not claim this is the optimal $z$.

\textbf{Checking the vector.} The claim $\gamma_2 \le 824443/1000000$ reduces, after clearing denominators, to integer comparisons: the feasibility inequality at every one of the 400 million states, and one comparison of logarithms carried out with exact rational enclosures. A deliberate negative control confirms the certificate is sharp: the same vector does \emph{not} prove the next smaller six-decimal target $0.824442$.

\section{Reproducibility}
\label{sec:repro}

Reproducibility is the point of this paper, so we describe the artifact exactly as it ships.

\textbf{What the verifier is.} \texttt{verify\_upper.py} is a single script that re-checks the claim from the stored certificate. It uses NumPy and Numba to apply the operator across 400 million states at reasonable speed; it is not a pure standard-library program. Every inequality that \emph{decides} the claim, however, is evaluated in Python integers and \texttt{Fraction}s: the per-state feasibility test is an integer comparison $S_i \le \Lambda V_i$ against the $\Lambda$ pinned in the script, and the final comparison $2\log(2z)/\log\lambda \le 824443/1000000$ is decided by exact rational enclosures of both logarithms with an explicit remainder bound. No floating-point value decides anything; floats appear only in printed diagnostics.

\textbf{What re-checking costs.} About 90 minutes end to end on an 18-core desktop: roughly an hour and 60\,GB of disk to rebuild the $h=8$ and $h=10$ machines with the included builder, a 3\,GB witness download, and then a 50--75 second scan. The often-quoted ``72 seconds'' is only that final scan.

\textbf{What the verifier checks.} SHA-256 authentication of the machines, the witness checkpoints, the certificate, and the frozen source paper; the feasibility inequality at every one of the 400{,}253{,}317 states; the exact rational log comparison; the hash-chained per-sweep log linking the 113 iterations and the exact rescale; a negative control (the same witness does \emph{not} prove $0.824442$); a sharpness control at $h=8$ (the calibration witness does not prove better than Lueker's published $0.826280$, so the pipeline is not silently optimistic); an adversarial control (a deliberately corrupted witness \emph{is} rejected at the per-state test); and a read-only assertion that the verifier modified no artifact.

\textbf{What the certificate cannot vouch for.} The theorem quantifies over the $h=10$ machine, so the machine's own correctness is a separate obligation, and hash-pinning authenticates bytes rather than semantics. The dangerous direction is a spurious rejection: the operator drops rejected columns from the row sum, so a legitimate transition wrongly marked as rejecting would understate $A_z$ and make the bound look better than it is. We therefore validate the builder against an independent pure-Python transcription of Lueker's Figure~7 (\texttt{upper\_oracle.py}) in two ways, via \texttt{verify\_graph\_oracle.py}: exhaustively for $h \le 6$ (every state, every column, up to 1.98 million transitions at $h=6$), and by uniformly sampling states of the actual $h=10$ machine and recomputing their complete 38-column rows with the oracle. Both agree with zero mismatches. This is strong evidence rather than proof: the sample covers a small fraction of the machine, and a common-mode misreading of Figure~7 shared by the builder and the oracle would escape both. The $h=8$ calibration reproducing Lueker's published constant is independent evidence against exactly that failure mode.

\textbf{Contents of the release.} The verifier, the certificate ($v$, $z$, $\lambda$, hashes), the graph builder and power iteration used to produce it, the oracle and the cross-check tool, unit tests for the engine and the oracle, the campaign log restricted to the sweep records the verifier authenticates, and this manuscript.

\section{What we could not do, and a correction}

Two negative findings from the same campaign are worth stating. First, on the lower bound: the same hardware certifies $\gamma_2 \ge 21{,}902{,}062/27{,}728{,}247 = 0.7898826$ at memory length $\ell = 17$, reproducing the published table exactly, but the current record $0.792665992$ \cite{heineman2024} was computed at $\ell = 21$, which requires external-memory techniques beyond a 128\,GB machine; we document the exact wall. Second, a recent machine-generated claim of $\gamma_2 \ge 0.79970$ circulating in a public bounds repository is a Monte-Carlo statement, not a theorem: replaying its own released data gives a failure probability of $1.0009\times10^{-12}$ (slightly worse than advertised) and the correctly rounded five-decimal value is $0.79969$.

\section{Discussion}

The interval for $\gamma_2$ is now $[0.792665992,\, 0.824443]$. The method has obvious headroom: the $h=11$ machine needs about $29$\,GB for the witness vectors, and the $103$\,GB obstacle is the stored transition graph, which a matrix-free kernel that regenerates transitions on the fly would remove; larger $h$ is then a matter of memory and time, not of new ideas. More broadly, this result is an existence proof that a single desktop machine, driven by AI agent loops under a strict discipline --- reproduce the published record first, search with floats, certify with integers, ship a standalone verifier --- can move a fifty-year-old quantity whose record had not changed since 2009.
